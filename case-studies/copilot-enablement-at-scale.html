The Challenge

Rolling out an enterprise AI license is easy. Getting thousands of people to actually change how they work is not. Applied Materials needed Copilot adoption that held up past the launch-week spike, at a scale where no single training session, email, or executive mandate could reach everyone who needed it.

The risk with a rollout this size isn't that people won't click "activate." It's that activation gets mistaken for adoption. Without a deliberate strategy, a license this powerful becomes just another tab people open once and forget, and the org ends up with an impressive activation number and no actual behavior change underneath it.

The Approach: The Enablement Flywheel

Rather than a single training push, the rollout ran on a four-phase methodology designed to build momentum instead of spending it in one launch event.

Phase 1

Listen

Understand the real barriers, not the assumed ones. Before designing any training, the work started with finding out where people actually got stuck with AI tools, whether that was confidence, relevance to their specific role, or simply not knowing where to start, rather than guessing from the top down.

Phase 2

Enable

Meet people where they are with relevant training. This is where the 70+ training sessions came in: role-specific, hands-on, and built around real workflows instead of generic feature tours, so the training matched how a given team actually used their time.

A recurring theme in those sessions: people expected AI to get things right on the first try, the same way they'd judge a piece of software. Once training reframed working with Copilot as iterative, more like collaborating with a colleague than querying a calculator, the friction dropped and people kept coming back.

The aha moment for me was so simple. I understood that iteration is key when working with AI. People treat AI like it should always be perfect, when it's just like working with humans.

— Ale Tamez
Phase 3

Measure

Track behavior change, not just login counts. Activation numbers are easy to celebrate and easy to misread. The program measured for continued, voluntary use, the signal that people were actually finding Copilot useful rather than just compliant.

Phase 4

Amplify

Turn early wins into organizational momentum. This is where the Champions network did its work, taking adoption from a program run by one team to a habit sustained by peers across the org.

The Champions Network

The 200+ person Champions network was the mechanism that let this scale past what any central team could deliver alone. Champions weren't just advocates; they ran the actual infrastructure that kept adoption alive between formal training sessions:

  • Open office hours so people could get unstuck without waiting for the next scheduled session
  • Living FAQs that captured real questions as they came up, instead of a static launch-day document
  • SOPs that turned one person's discovery into a repeatable practice for their whole team
  • Gamification that made continued use something people wanted to keep up with, not another compliance checkbox

Peer-to-peer enablement carries more weight than a mandate from L&D. A Champion sitting two desks away who can show a colleague how they use Copilot in their own daily work converts skeptics that a company-wide email never will.

The Result

The program reached 97% adoption across 10,000+ users, not as a launch-day spike, but as sustained, voluntary use tracked through the Measure phase of the Flywheel. The 200+ person Champions network continues to run the open hours, FAQs, SOPs, and gamification infrastructure that keeps that number holding, well past the initial rollout.

Adoption is a metric. Bravery is a strategy. The number that matters isn't how many people activated a tool, it's how many kept choosing to use it once no one was watching.
